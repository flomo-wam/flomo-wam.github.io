3D Scene Flow as a World-Action Model Intermediate for Learning from Human Video

  1. 01

    1 / 8 · Motivation

    [1/8] We’re introducing FloMo, a world-action model designed to make action-free egocentric human video more useful for robot learning. FloMo jointly predicts 3D scene flow and robot actions. Human clips supervise scene flow without action labels; robot demonstrations connect the same motion target to control. Most video-pretrained policies predict future RGB frames. We instead predict how points in the scene move in 3D. 🧵
  2. 02

    2 / 8 · Representation

    [2/8] 3D scene flow records the cumulative 3D displacement of tracked points, expressed in the first camera frame. This removes appearance details such as texture and lighting while retaining the motion relevant to action. It also avoids a limitation of 2D optical flow, which mixes object motion with camera motion. Because it represents how the scene moves rather than whether a hand or gripper caused that motion, scene flow can carry manipulation knowledge across embodiments.   3. 03

    3 / 8 · Model and training

    [3/8] FloMo reuses a pretrained Wan 2.2 5B video model rather than adding a separate motion encoder. We render the 3D motion field as color, pass it through the frozen video encoder, and fine-tune one backbone to predict motion and robot actions together. That shared target connects two data sources: action-free egocentric human video supplies motion supervision, while robot demonstrations connect motion to action. We first train across both sources, then fine-tune on robot data alone. At test time, an image and language instruction produce a 16-action chunk.

  4. 04

    4 / 8 · In-distribution results

    [4/8] We first evaluated three contact-rich, bimanual manipulation tasks represented in the robot training distribution. FloMo performed best on every task and averaged 87% success. A joint video–action baseline averaged 47%, the FloMo variant trained to predict both motion and future RGB averaged 40%, and behavior cloning without video pretraining averaged 7%. Adding future-RGB prediction did not improve the policy; it substantially reduced success.
  5. 05

    5 / 8 · OOD results

    [5/8] We then tested three kinds of generalization beyond the robot demonstrations: a type of movement, a task, and an object that were absent from the robot data. All three were represented in the egocentric human videos, but not in the robot demonstrations. FloMo succeeded in 12 of 15 rollouts, or 80%. The matching variant that also predicted future RGB succeeded in 0 of 15. The rollouts below show the robot closing a cabinet with a new movement, pulling a toy’s string in a new task, and placing a previously unseen object in a bowl.

  6. 06

    6 / 8 · Analysis

    [6/8] Two controlled analyses help explain the result. In an inverse-dynamics probe, 3D scene flow reached the same action-prediction performance as RGB at full data scale. With only 1% of the data, it reduced action error by 22%. When RGB and scene flow were both available, action tokens assigned about 3× more attention to scene flow across the transformer. Scene flow contains less visual information, but preserves a more direct signal for action.
  7. 07

    7 / 8 · Egocentric human video

    [7/8] The main transfer result is that 3D motion makes action-free egocentric human video substantially more useful for robot learning. Human hands and robot grippers look different, but they can produce the same motion in the objects they manipulate. Holding the architecture and motion target fixed, removing only ~81 hours of egocentric human video reduced average OOD success from 80% to 7%. This isolates the role of the human data: it supplied most of the generalization to movements, tasks, and objects absent from the robot demonstrations. On a string-pulling task present only in human video, FloMo completed 3/5 trials, compared with 1/5 for DreamZero and 0/5 for behavior cloning.

  8. 08

    8 / 8 · Conclusion

    [8/8] FloMo changes what a world-action model learns to predict. The video backbone retains the benefit of pretraining, but its downstream target is 3D motion rather than future pixels. That distinction allows action-free egocentric human video to contribute directly to robot learning: human clips teach the model how manipulation changes a scene; robot demonstrations teach it which robot actions produce those changes. In our experiments, this was a more effective route from human video to robot action than future-video prediction. Paper and videos: https://flomo-wam.github.io
