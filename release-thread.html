3D Scene Flow as a World-Action Model Intermediate for Learning from Human Video

  1. 01

    1 / 8 · Motivation

    [1/8] We’re introducing FloMo, a world-action model that learns from human and robot video by predicting 3D scene flow together with robot actions. Most video-pretrained policies predict future RGB frames. We instead predict how points in the scene move in 3D. On real-robot manipulation, this target improves task success and generalization beyond the robot training distribution. 🧵
  2. 02

    2 / 8 · Representation

    [2/8] 3D scene flow records the cumulative 3D displacement of tracked points, expressed in the first camera frame. This removes appearance details such as texture and lighting while retaining the motion relevant to action. It also avoids a limitation of 2D optical flow, which mixes object motion with camera motion. The examples below show the RGB observation, point tracks, and corresponding 3D motion field.

  3. 03

    3 / 8 · Model and training

    [3/8] FloMo reuses a pretrained Wan 2.2 5B video model rather than adding a separate motion encoder. We render the 3D motion field as color, pass it through the frozen video encoder, and fine-tune one backbone to predict motion and robot actions together. Training has two stages. First, human videos provide motion supervision while robot demonstrations provide motion and action supervision. We then fine-tune on robot data alone. At test time, an image and language instruction produce a 16-action chunk.

  4. 04

    4 / 8 · In-distribution results

    [4/8] We first evaluated three contact-rich, bimanual manipulation tasks represented in the robot training distribution. FloMo performed best on every task and averaged 87% success. A joint video–action baseline averaged 47%, the FloMo variant trained to predict both motion and future RGB averaged 40%, and behavior cloning without video pretraining averaged 7%. Adding future-RGB prediction did not improve the policy; it substantially reduced success.
  5. 05

    5 / 8 · OOD results

    [5/8] We then tested three kinds of generalization beyond the robot demonstrations: a type of movement, a task, and an object that were absent from the robot data. FloMo succeeded in 12 of 15 rollouts, or 80%. The matching variant that also predicted future RGB succeeded in 0 of 15. The rollouts below show the robot closing a cabinet with a new movement, pulling a toy’s string in a new task, and placing a previously unseen object in a bowl.

  6. 06

    6 / 8 · Analysis

    [6/8] Two controlled analyses help explain the result. In an inverse-dynamics probe, 3D scene flow reached the same action-prediction performance as RGB at full data scale. With only 1% of the data, it reduced action error by 22%. When RGB and scene flow were both available, action tokens assigned about 3× more attention to scene flow across the transformer. Scene flow contains less visual information, but preserves a more direct signal for action.
  7. 07

    7 / 8 · Human-video transfer

    [7/8] The transfer depends on action-free human video. Human hands and robot grippers look different, but can produce similar 3D motion in the objects they manipulate. When we removed roughly 81 hours of human video while keeping the architecture and motion target fixed, average success on the three out-of-distribution evaluations fell from 80% to 7%. This ablation attributes most of the generalization gain to motion supervision from human activity video.

  8. 08

    8 / 8 · Example and takeaway

    [8/8] Pulling a toy’s string was present in the human videos but absent from the robot demonstrations. FloMo completed the task in 3/5 trials; DreamZero completed 1/5; behavior cloning without pretraining completed 0/5. Across these experiments, 3D motion was a more effective intermediate between human video and robot action than future-pixel reconstruction. Paper and videos: https://flomo-wam.github.io
