3D Scene Flow as a World-Action Model Intermediate for Learning from Human Video

  1. 01

    1 / 8 · Motivation

    [1/8] We’re introducing FloMo, a world-action model designed to make action-free egocentric human video more useful for robot learning. FloMo jointly predicts 3D scene flow and robot actions. Human clips supervise scene flow without action labels; robot demonstrations connect the same motion target to control. Most video-pretrained policies predict future RGB frames. We instead predict how points in the scene move in 3D. 🧵
  2. 02

    2 / 8 · Representation

    [2/8] 3D scene flow tracks how points in a scene move through 3D space. This removes appearance details such as texture and lighting while retaining the motion relevant to action. It also avoids a limitation of 2D optical flow, which mixes object motion with camera motion. Because it represents how the scene moves rather than whether a hand or gripper caused that motion, scene flow can carry manipulation knowledge across embodiments.   3. 03

    3 / 8 · Model and training

    [3/8] FloMo reuses a pretrained Wan 2.2 5B video model rather than adding a separate motion encoder. We render the 3D motion field as color, pass it through the frozen video encoder, and finetune one backbone to predict motion and robot actions together. That shared target connects two data sources: action-free egocentric human video supplies motion supervision, while robot demonstrations connect motion to action. We first train across both sources, then finetune on robot data alone.

  4. 04

    4 / 8 · In-distribution results

    [4/8] We first evaluated three tasks from the robot training distribution: “Open the oven, retrieve the croissant, place it on a plate”; “Open the hinged lid, grasp the screwdriver, place it inside”; and “Grasp the corn cob and place it in the bowl.” FloMo performed best on every task and averaged 87% success. A joint video–action baseline averaged 47%, the FloMo variant trained to predict both motion and future RGB averaged 40%, and behavior cloning without video pretraining averaged 7%. Adding future-RGB prediction did not improve the policy; it substantially reduced success.
  5. 05

    5 / 8 · OOD results

    [5/8] We then tested three kinds of generalization beyond the robot demonstrations: a type of movement, a task, and an object that were absent from the robot data. All three were represented in the egocentric human videos, but not in the robot demonstrations. FloMo succeeded in 12 of 15 rollouts, or 80%. The matching variant that also predicted future RGB succeeded in 0 of 15. The rollouts below cover each case: push the cabinet door shut; pull the string on the toy in the air; and place the unseen highlighter in the bowl.

  6. 06

    6 / 8 · Analysis

    [6/8] Two controlled analyses help explain the result. In an inverse-dynamics probe, 3D scene flow reached the same action-prediction performance as RGB at full data scale. With only 1% of the data, it reduced action error by 22%. When RGB and scene flow were both available, action tokens assigned about 3× more attention to scene flow across the transformer. Scene flow contains less visual information, but preserves a more direct signal for action.
  7. 07

    7 / 8 · Egocentric human video

    [7/8] 3D scene flow lets FloMo learn transferable manipulation knowledge from action-free egocentric human video. With the architecture and motion target fixed, removing the ~81 hours of human video dropped average OOD success from 80% to 7%—showing that human video supplied most of the generalization beyond the robot demonstrations.

  8. 08

    8 / 8 · Conclusion

    [8/8] FloMo changes what a world-action model learns to predict. The video backbone retains the benefit of pretraining, but its downstream target is 3D motion rather than future pixels. That distinction allows action-free egocentric human video to contribute directly to robot learning: human clips teach the model how manipulation changes a scene; robot demonstrations teach it which robot actions produce those changes. In our experiments, this was a more effective route from human video to robot action than future-video prediction. Paper and videos: https://flomo-wam.github.io
